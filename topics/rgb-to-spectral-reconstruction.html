Abstract

Spectral imaging reveals tissue characteristics invisible to standard RGB cameras, making it valuable for early disease detection in medical endoscopy. However, hyperspectral sensors cannot fit inside swallowable capsule cameras due to size and power constraints. This research investigates whether spectral information can be reconstructed from standard RGB images using both classical physics-based methods and machine learning approaches.

I evaluated two approaches: (1) a multi-filter method using 16 optical filters with ridge regularization, validated against spectrometer measurements, and (2) a neural network trained on the SPECTRALPACA medical dataset achieving R² = 0.994 for cross-subject generalization within palm tissue.

While ML methods show excellent in-domain performance, stress testing with synthetic perfect red images revealed that models trained only on healthy tissue hallucinate spectral features when encountering unfamiliar inputs. This highlights a critical safety concern for clinical deployment: models learn domain-specific patterns rather than universal optical physics.

Background

The Problem: Clinical Needs vs Hardware Constraints

Wireless Capsule Endoscopy (WCE) revolutionized gastrointestinal diagnosis by allowing patients to swallow a vitamin-pill-sized camera that travels through the digestive tract. However, these capsules use standard RGB cameras that miss subtle spectral details crucial for detecting early tissue abnormalities like changes in vascular structure or oxygenation levels.

RGB vs Spectral Imaging

RGB cameras capture only three broad color bands, compressing wavelength information into a "summary" of light. In contrast, spectral imaging captures many narrow wavelength bands (often 16-200), preserving the full spectral "fingerprint" of materials. This fingerprint reveals:

  • Distinct signatures for visually similar tissues
  • Oxygenation levels in blood vessels
  • Early pathological changes invisible to RGB cameras

RGB integrates light across broad bands vs spectral imaging resolving full reflectance curves

Key Challenge: Hyperspectral cameras are too large and power-hungry for capsule miniaturization. Can we mathematically recover spectral data from standard RGB images?

Research Questions

  1. Classical reconstruction: Can multi-filter imaging reconstruct spectral curves matching spectrometer measurements?
  2. ML performance: Can neural networks accurately predict spectral signatures from RGB inputs?
  3. Generalization: How do models perform on inputs different from training data?
Goal: Develop mapping f: ℝ³(RGB) → ℝ¹⁶⁺(Spectrum) to reconstruct spectral signatures from RGB images.

Methodology

Study 1: Classical Multi-Filter Approach

  • 16 optical filters with known transmission characteristics
  • 16 filters × 3 RGB channels = 48 measurements per sample
  • Ridge regularization for inverse problem solving
  • Validated against spectrometer ground truth

Filter transmission curve across visible spectrum (400-700 nm)

Study 2: Machine Learning Approach (SPECTRALPACA)

  • SPECTRALPACA: Hyperspectral videos of human palm tissue, 10 participants
  • 16 wavelength bands (465-641 nm), 272×512 pixel resolution
  • Split: Subjects 1-5 training (500K pixels), Subject 6 testing (100K pixels)
  • Limitation: Synthetic RGB (idealized, no camera noise)
  • Architecture: MLP with attention - Input(3) → FC(512) → FC(256) → FC(128) → Output(16)
  • Loss: Combined MSE, Weighted MSE, and Spectral Angle Mapper

Study 3: Generalization Stress Test

Applied model to synthetic "perfect red" image (RGB: [253, 6, 14]) - far outside training distribution.

Analysis

Comparison: Classical vs Machine Learning

Feature Multi-Filter (Classical) Machine Learning
Mechanism Physics-based (inverse problem) Data-driven (learned patterns)
Hardware Complex (16 filters) Simple (standard RGB)
In-Domain Accuracy Good (matches spectrometer shape) Excellent (R² = 0.994)
Generalization Robust (based on physics) Fragile (data-dependent)
Capsule Suitability Low (size constraints) High (standard camera)
Fine Details Smoothed (regularization) Better preservation

Key Insights

  • Both approaches demonstrate RGB-to-spectral reconstruction is feasible
  • ML achieves R² > 0.99 when test matches training distribution
  • Models hallucinate on unfamiliar inputs - potential clinical hazard
  • Trade-off: ML enables miniaturization but lacks physics-based robustness

Limitations

  • Synthetic RGB without camera noise or compression artifacts
  • Only healthy palm tissue - no pathological samples or GI tissue
  • Limited to 16 bands (465-641nm) vs full hyperspectral

Challenges

  • Underdetermined inverse problem: 16+ spectral values from 3 RGB measurements
  • Edge wavelength errors at boundaries (465nm, 641nm)
  • Limited training diversity: only healthy palm tissue
  • Hallucination on unfamiliar inputs could cause misdiagnosis
  • Need pathological data: tumors, inflammation, bleeding, GI tissues
  • Validation required with real capsule camera images (noise, compression)

Future Work

  • Collect hyperspectral data from actual GI tissue including pathological conditions
  • Test with real capsule camera images (noise, compression, aberrations)
  • Implement uncertainty quantification and out-of-distribution detection
  • Expand to full hyperspectral range (60+ bands, 400-700nm)
  • Clinical validation studies with gastroenterology departments
  • Explore physics-informed neural networks and ensemble methods
