The Task

Google's Quick, Draw! dataset contains over 50 million hand-drawn doodles across 300+ categories. The challenge: build a CNN that can classify a subset of 10 deliberately confusing categories: round objects that look remarkably similar even to the human eye.

The 10 categories: Apple, Baseball, Basketball, Blueberry, Circle, Clock, Cookie, Donut, Face, Pizza.

Data

The dataset consists of 28x28 grayscale doodle images split into training, validation, and test sets (50,000 test samples). All classes are well-balanced, so no resampling or weighting was needed.

Why these categories? Round objects like baseballs, blueberries, circles, and donuts create genuine ambiguity, testing how well the CNN can pick up on subtle structural differences in hand-drawn sketches.

Data augmentation (rotation, flipping, scaling) was considered to combat overfitting and improve generalization, though the large dataset size already provided sufficient diversity for training.

Model Architecture

A custom CNN built in PyTorch with three convolutional layers, max-pooling, dropout regularization, and fully connected layers:

CNN architecture: 1@28x28 input through three convolutional blocks to 10-class output

  • Conv1: 1 → 16 filters (3x3, padding=1)
  • Conv2: 16 → 32 filters (3x3, padding=1), then MaxPool2d
  • Conv3: 32 → 32 filters (3x3, padding=1), then MaxPool2d
  • FC1: 1568 → 256 with Dropout(0.5)
  • FC2: 256 → 10 (softmax output)

All hidden layers use ReLU activation. Total trainable parameters: ~418,000.

Architecture tweak: Grouping conv1+conv2 before the first pooling layer (instead of pooling after every conv) improved accuracy from 88.8% to 90.6%.

Training

The model was trained on GPU with the following configuration:

  • Optimizer: Adam (lr=0.001)
  • Loss: Cross-entropy
  • Batch size: 64
  • Epochs: Tuned to break the 90% accuracy barrier

Training and validation loss/accuracy curves were monitored to detect overfitting. The dropout layer (p=0.5) before the final classifier was the primary regularization mechanism.

Loss and accuracy curves over 15 epochs. Validation loss begins to plateau around epoch 8, while training loss continues to decrease, a mild sign of overfitting kept in check by dropout.

Results

The model achieved strong performance on visually ambiguous doodle categories:

  • Training accuracy: 90.62%
  • Validation accuracy: 90.08%
  • Test accuracy: ~90%

The small gap between training and validation accuracy suggests the model generalizes well without severe overfitting.

Hardest categories: Baseball, Blueberry, Circle, and Face were the most commonly confused, all sharing a dominant circular shape in doodle form.

Analysis

Prediction analysis on individual samples revealed interesting confidence patterns:

Two baseball doodles with very different confidence levels. The top sample is predicted at 62% confidence; the bottom one drops to 28% with Blueberry nearly tied, illustrating how ambiguous simple sketches can be.

  • For clear doodles, the model predicted correctly with ~62% confidence, with the runner-up class (usually another round object) at ~19%
  • For ambiguous doodles, confidence dropped to ~28% with near-ties between similar categories

The confusion between round objects (Baseball vs. Blueberry vs. Circle vs. Face) highlights a fundamental limitation: when hand-drawn sketches reduce objects to their simplest geometric form, even sophisticated CNNs struggle to distinguish them without additional context like size or internal details.

Takeaways

  • 90% accuracy on deliberately confusing categories validates that even a modest CNN can learn meaningful features from simple doodles
  • Architectural decisions matter: grouping convolutions before pooling preserved spatial detail and yielded a ~2% accuracy gain
  • Dropout at 0.5 was effective at keeping the train/val gap small despite the model's 418K parameters
  • The per-class confusion patterns provide intuitive insight into what the network actually learns to distinguish
